# Threat Taxonomy  
Online survey “fraud” arises from diverse sources, each requiring different detection tactics. Automated bots (form-filling scripts, headless browsers) are one class, human click-farm workers (often using VPNs/proxies) another. There is also intentional misrepresentation: eligibility fraud (e.g. adults posing as minors, cisgender as gender-minority), duplicate or ballot-box stuffing (same individual responding many times), and mischievous responders who give extreme or contradictory answers on purpose. Careless or low-effort responses (fatigue, inattentiveness) form a separate threat. Crucially, recent work shows *humans*, not bots, often drive bad data in social-media recruitment: Jaffe *et al.* (2026) report that in large online panel studies most invalid responses came from fraudulent human participants (often outside the target country) rather than bots. Emerging threats include autonomous LLM “respondents” and LLM-assisted humans pasting generated text (Westwood 2025, PNAS) – both raising the bar for content-based checks.  Empirical reviews find very high variability in fraud prevalence: Comachio *et al.* (2025) report that in reviewed health-research surveys 3–94% of respondents were excluded as fraud, with time-to-complete checks (e.g. <5 min) used in ~26% of studies. In practice, open social-media recruitment of adolescents (14–17) with incentives can be especially vulnerable: Pinzón *et al.* (2024) documented usable response rates collapsing from 75% to 10% in incentive-based surveys due to fraud. 

# Signal Families  
We organize detection signals into families as requested. 

## Paradata and Telemetry  
Web-survey paradata – response timings, keystroke/mouse dynamics, browser focus events, and automation flags – can flag abnormal behavior.  For example, very short completion times (“speeders”) are a red flag. Pinzón *et al.* (2024) tested speed thresholds: flagging respondents taking ≤30–50% of median time identified many fraudsters. Hidden honeypot items (questions invisible to humans but seen by bots) are another approach.  More sophisticated telemetry includes mouse movement patterns and keystroke intervals; CloudResearch reports that analyzing “how” (mouse trajectories, typing cadence) rather than “what” respondents answer can detect AI-driven fraud with very high accuracy. Headless-browser signals (e.g. `navigator.webdriver` flag, Chrome DevTools Protocol anomalies) are also used, as is device attestation (Google’s reCAPTCHA Enterprise scores or Apple’s Private Access Tokens). Qualtrics’ built-in “bot detection” uses reCAPTCHA V3 to score the likelihood of a bot without user interaction. In sum, paradata checks can detect scripted bots and very inattentive humans; they require no labels (unsupervised heuristics) and tend to be resilient to LLM text generation (which does not speed up form-filling by bots). However, they can be intrusive (keystroke logs) and can raise privacy concerns with minors (fingerprinting vulnerabilities).  

## Network and Device Signals  
Network-level features check IP/geolocation and device properties.  Standard tools (MaxMind GeoIP, IPQualityScore, Scamalytics, IPHub) classify IP addresses as datacenter, VPN, proxy or mobile carrier, or flag high “fraud risk”. For instance, Pinzón *et al.* used IPHub to mark VPNs and MaxMind’s MinFraud score (above 59%) to flag suspicious sessions. Consistency checks compare IP-derived location/timezone to self-reported demographics: Jaffe *et al.* recommend blocking responses with obvious discordance (e.g. respondent claims US residency but IP is foreign).  Browser fingerprinting (FingerprintJS, AmIUnique) can help detect repeated submissions from the same machine even under different IPs. These methods require no labeled data. Independent accuracy evaluations are scant: vendors claim high accuracy in detecting datacenter/VPN IPs, but researchers caution no tool is foolproof (e.g. mobile carriers often have IPs marked “datacenter”, and savvy fraudsters can avoid known proxy pools). In practice, network signals excel at catching obvious foreign or automated traffic but can produce false positives for legitimate privacy users (VPNs, Tor) or mobile gamers.  

## Identity Signals  
Simple identity heuristics can catch fraud when contact info is collected. Examples include disposable email detection and pattern scoring of email/phone. Pinzón *et al.* created an **email-address score** by spotting unnatural patterns (excessive digits, random strings) – responses scoring ≥2 on that metric were flagged. They also checked if email addresses bounced on re-contact (with an email verification service). Regular expressions or domain blacklists identify throwaway domains. Similarly, requiring a valid phone or doing re-contact (“verification-call” or email challenge) can confirm authenticity. Note however that minors may lack stable email/phone or use parental contacts, complicating this approach. These signals require minimal supervision and can be implemented easily (libraries exist to check email validity/disposability). Their performance is typically high in recall (since fraudsters often use bogus email) but depends on collecting email/phone in the survey; they cannot catch careful fraudsters using real credentials.  

## Closed-ended Content Consistency  
Analysis of responses to structured items can reveal implausibility. Classic checks include long-string (straightlining in matrix questions), even-odd split-half consistency, instructed-response items (attention checks), and logically inconsistent pairs. For example, one might flag respondents who answer “always” to long matrices or fail obvious reverse-coded items. Psychometric person-fit statistics (e.g. van der Flier’s *lz* or *lz*²) compare an individual’s response pattern against an IRT model to catch aberrant behavior. Cimpian’s “mischievous responder” approach uses low-base-rate items (e.g. improbable drug use) to find respondents endorsing unlikely combinations. Mahalanobis distance can flag multivariate outliers across multiple items. All these are unsupervised signals (aside from known trap items) and are standard in survey-quality toolkits. They are effective against inattentive or random responders but have limitations: sophisticated bots or LLMs might mimic plausible answer distributions. Equally, genuine but atypical individuals (e.g. a nonbinary adolescent selecting rare combinations) can be falsely flagged if checks are poorly designed.  

## Open-ended (Text) Content  
Free-text answers provide rich signals. Basic duplicate-checking (MinHash/SimHash on text) finds verbatim copy/paste. Embedding-based similarity (e.g. sentence transformers) can detect near-duplicates or homogeneous writing style across respondents. Stylometric analysis (text length, vocabulary richness, punctuation usage) can reveal unnatural consistency if many respondents sound identical. Many teams now try LLM-specific detectors (OpenAI’s DetectGPT, ICLR’s Fast-DetectGPT, Kaggle’s “binoculars” ensemble, or simpler features like perplexity) but these are fragile: adversarial prompting and RL can fool them, as demonstrated by the RAID benchmark. For example, Pinzón *et al.* found that early fraud waves produced formulaic, plagiaristic text (often copied from the survey itself), which future respondents evaded by crafting more subtle narratives.  In short, open-text checks are powerful when fraud is evident (exact copy, canned wording), but we have little assurance they’ll detect a well-instructed LLM or human imitating human writing. No public studies (as of 2024) report robust real-world performance for text-based fraud detectors in surveys. 

## Cross-respondent Structure  
Examining the joint structure of responses can catch coordinated fraud. For example, constructing a graph where respondents are connected if they share IP, email, or very similar answers lets one detect “crowds” of suspicious people (e.g. community detection might reveal one fraudster controlling multiple entries). Time-series bursts (many submissions minutes apart) or distributional anomalies (e.g. identical extreme ratings from many) also hint at farm activity. These methods are largely unsupervised and more common in fraud analytics than published survey literature. One could apply social-network sybil-detection ideas here. In practice, such graph- and distribution-based techniques show promise (e.g. Prolific and MTurk panels internally use some of these) but quantitative performance data are scarce in the academic literature.

# Modeling and Fusion Approaches  
We now consider how to combine signals into a decision.  

- **Rule-based Ensembles.** A simple approach is to set hard rules (e.g. require failing ≥2 indicators to mark fraud). This “multiple-hurdle” strategy was used by Pratt-Chapman *et al.* (2021), who dropped any respondent meeting two or more fraud criteria; they ended up excluding 1,408 of 1,977 surveys. Pinzón *et al.* (2024) built additive and logical-rule ensembles of their 31 indicators (e.g. any respondent flagged by IRV inconsistency *or* IP-VPN *or* copied answers). Such ensembles can achieve high recall (catch most fraud) but at the cost of false positives: Pinzón found that the best ensembles still had “unacceptable” false-positive error rates (often >5–10%). Rule ensembles require no training data but yield unknown error rates unless calibrated. They are easy to implement (just logical conditions) and often the starting point in survey research, but they lack a probabilistic output and can be brittle if fraudsters adapt their behavior.  

- **Supervised Classification.** Given a labeled sample of fraud vs. valid responses, one can train a classifier (gradient-boosting, random forest, neural net). Schroeders *et al.* (2022) demonstrated such models on intentionally labeled survey fraud. These models can flexibly weight all signals and interactions. Reported performance in the literature is mixed and depends on the ground truth: high AUROC/precision on synthetic or cleaned datasets, but likely over-optimistic if labels are noisy. Supervised methods require a training set of confirmed fraud (rare in general surveys), and they will only learn patterns present in that set. They can be implemented in Python via scikit-learn or PyTorch; major libraries exist. Maturity: several preprints and dissertations (e.g. Gordon *et al.* 2026) explore this, but peer-reviewed evidence on generalizability is lacking. These models are likely fragile to novel fraud tactics (e.g. a new SMS recruitment site) unless retrained. 

- **Positive-Unlabeled (PU) Learning / Semi-supervised.** In most surveys we know only a few fraud cases (seeded bots or spotted bad responses) while the rest are unlabeled mix. PU-learning treats the small “positive” set (labeled fraud) and large unlabeled pool to infer a decision boundary. This requires strong assumptions (e.g. labeled frauds are representative) and complex methods (biased SVM, two-step, etc). The literature has sparse examples in survey fraud, but analogous methods in spam detection suggest it can improve over naive unsupervised filters. Implementation can use existing PU-learning Python code (e.g. `sklearnpu` or custom EM). This approach is promising when some gold-standard fraud is available, but its success depends on label quality and prevalence assumptions. 

- **Unsupervised Anomaly Detection.** Methods like Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, or autoencoders can score each response on “anomaly”. These require no labels and work on numeric features (e.g. times, metadata). There are no published benchmarks of these methods for survey fraud specifically, but one could adapt them. They can flag unusual surveys beyond simple rules. However, unsupervised anomalies often flag any minority subgroup (even legitimate) and lack interpretability. Implementation via scikit-learn is straightforward, but usage in survey research is experimental. 

- **Latent Class / Mixture Models (No Gold Standard).** A powerful Bayesian approach is to model each fraud-check as a (noisy) diagnostic test in a latent-class framework. E.g. assume two latent classes (“valid” vs “fraudulent”) and model each indicator’s sensitivity/specificity (Hui–Walter or Dawid–Skene formulations). Identifiability typically requires at least 3 independent checks (or at least 2 with differing prevalences in subgroups). Extensions allow conditional dependence (Dendukuri–Joseph) or random-effects LCM to account for correlation among indicators. One can incorporate covariates on fraud prevalence (latent-class regression) or “anchor” cases in semi-supervised LCMs. The estimands are the latent-class prevalences and item-error rates; identification relies on missing-data/statistical assumptions (e.g. conditional independence of checks given class). Stan, PyMC, or NumPyro can implement these models – one defines a latent binary fraud variable for each respondent and Bernoulli models for each check’s outcome. The output is a posterior probability of fraud per respondent. This approach (often called “no gold standard” diagnostic meta-analysis) is well-studied in epidemiology but rare in survey-fraud literature. Its advantage is principled uncertainty and ability to fuse multiple imperfect signals. Challenges include label-switching, specifying priors for many error rates, and verifying independence assumptions. To our knowledge there are no published applications of LCMs to survey fraud yet, but it is a promising avenue for probabilistic scoring. 

- **Response-Time Mixture / Mixture-IRT Models.** Recent psychometric models explicitly capture careless responding by assuming a mixture of “attentive” and “careless” response processes. For example, Ulitzsch *et al.* (2022, Psychometrika) propose a latent-mixture IRT where each respondent has a probability of being in a careless state; careless responses may have lower discrimination or a separate (uniform/random) component. Changepoint models (Welz & Alfons) detect when a respondent switches into careless mode during a long survey. The estimands are the mixture weights and IRT parameters for each class. Identifiability can be tricky (requires distinguishing the mixture on data alone). Implementation would involve custom HMC/variational code in Stan/NumPyro, modeling item responses given a latent class for each person. These models directly yield a posterior “carelessness probability”. They are peer-reviewed (Ulitzsch et al. 2022) and have shown ability to detect insufficient effort; however, they focus on scale-based data and are less studied for fraud or adversarial respondents. 

- **Graph / Sybil Detection Methods.** Fraud rings might appear as clusters in a respondent similarity graph (nodes = respondents, edges for shared IP/email/answer patterns). One could apply community-detection or subgraph-identification algorithms to find densely connected groups. There is no off-the-shelf survey tool for this, but graph-analysis libraries (NetworkX, iGraph) can be used. This is akin to social-network Sybil detection in computer security. No mature evaluation exists for survey fraud, but it can catch coordinated attacks (e.g. a click-farm filling thousands of surveys). 

- **Sequential/Online Monitoring.** During data collection one can monitor incoming data for shifts. CUSUM or Bayesian change-point methods can be applied to the time series of average fraud-scores or flag-rates: e.g. detect an abrupt rise in the fraction of speeders. Drift detectors (ADWIN) can signal when respondent features distribution changes. There is little published specifically for survey fraud, but the approach is standard in streaming analytics. It requires defining a metric to track and setting control thresholds (with no gold standard, thresholds could be tuned on historical data). Implementation could use existing `ruptures` or custom code. This is a preventative strategy to stop data collection early if fraud spikes. 

- **Calibration and Decision Theory.** Once a fraud probability model is built, one must choose decision thresholds. Under asymmetric costs (removing a genuine teen is worse than missing a fraud), Neyman-Pearson lemma suggests controlling false positives at some α. Conformal prediction is an emerging idea: it could produce a set of flagged respondents with a guaranteed error bound. In practice, teams use heuristic “triage”: e.g. auto-accept low-risk (below threshold), auto-reject high-risk, and manually review the middle band. Some researchers have begun to incorporate group-fairness constraints (e.g. equal FPR across gender/race groups) when setting thresholds, to avoid biased exclusions. Finally, rather than hard classification, one can carry forward uncertainty: e.g. weight each response by its “probability valid” in later analysis, or use multiple imputation (sampling fraud labels) to propagate uncertainty. This research on integrating uncertainty from data cleaning is nascent.

# Five Promising Approaches (Models)  

Below we outline five model-based approaches in more detail, noting statistical specification, estimands, identifiability, and how one might implement them in Python (Stan/PyMC/NumPyro for Bayesian models).  

1. **Bayesian Latent Class Model (LCM)**. Treat each fraud-indicator as a noisy test for a latent binary “Fraud” status. For respondent *i*, let $z_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicate fraud (1=fraud). For each indicator *j*, model $X_{ij}\mid z_i\sim Bernoulli(p_{z_i,j})$ where $p_{1,j}= \Pr(X=1\mid\text{fraud})$ (sensitivity) and $p_{0,j}= \Pr(X=1\mid\text{valid})$ (false-positive rate). Place priors on the $p$’s and on $\pi=\Pr(z_i=1)$ (fraud prevalence). The estimands are the posterior $\Pr(z_i=1\mid X_i)$. **Identifiability**: Hui–Walter conditions require at least 2 indicators with different prevalence among subpopulations, or 3+ independent indicators in one population. Conditional independence of $X$’s given $z$ is also assumed (can be relaxed with covariance terms or by grouping dependent tests). In practice, one uses Stan/NumPyro to specify this model with latent $z_i$ (discrete support can be marginalized in PyMC3/NumPyro or handled via data-augmentation in Stan). For example, in PyMC: `z ~ Bernoulli(pi); X_j ~ Bernoulli( switch(z, p1_j, p0_j) )`. The posterior yields a fraud probability for each survey. This avoids needing “gold standard” fraud labels, but relies on correct priors and assumptions.  

2. **Probit Random-Effects Latent Class**. A continuous latent extension: assume an unobserved continuous liability $Y_i$ for “fraud propensity” with a threshold model. Let $z_i=1$ iff $Y_i>\tau$. Model $Y_i\sim Normal(\mu+\mathbf{w}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2)$ where $\mathbf{w}$ are covariates (e.g. IP outside country) affecting fraud prevalence. Then $X_{ij}\mid Y_i \sim Bernoulli(\Phi(a_j + b_j Y_i))$ linking each test to $Y_i$ (multivariate probit LCM). This **probit LCM** handles dependent indicators via the continuous factor. Identifiability still requires enough variation in $\mathbf{w}$ or multiple items. One can implement in NumPyro by sampling $Y_i$ (latent normal) and $z_i$ from it. This yields a richer posterior fraud score and can incorporate predictors.  

3. **Mixture-IRT for Careless Responding**. Assume respondents come from two latent classes: *Attentive* and *Careless*. For each item *j*, let an IRT (e.g. 2PL) model hold for attentive respondents. For careless ones, assume a separate response distribution (e.g. a uniform guess or a low-discrimination IRT). Formally, introduce latent $c_i\in\{0,1\}$ for carelessness. If $c_i=0$, the probability of a correct (or particular Likert) response on item *j* is $\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(a_j \theta_i - d_j)$; if $c_i=1$, it might be $\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(a_j^\prime \theta_i^\prime - d_j^\prime)$ with $a_j^\prime\approx0$ (nearly random). Place priors on $a_j,d_j$ and on class prevalence $\Pr(c_i=1)=\gamma$. The estimand is the posterior $\Pr(c_i=1\mid\text{data})$. Identifiability: one typically anchors the scale by the attentive class; carelessness is inferred only if pattern fits that well. Ulitzsch *et al.* report that with moderate test length this can work. In Stan/PyMC, one can code a mixture: sample $c_i\sim Bernoulli(\gamma)$ and then item responses conditional on $c_i$. The model is similar to a 2-class IRT mixture. A variant is a **changepoint model**: allow $c_{i,t}$ to switch from 0 to 1 at some item index *t*. That is a discrete changepoint to infer when inattention starts. This adds complexity but can also be done in Stan via marginalizing the unknown switch point.  

4. **Graphical Community Detection**. Construct a graph $G$ where each node is a respondent and edges connect respondents sharing suspicious features (e.g. same IP on the same day, identical email or high text-similarity). Fraud rings may appear as clusters with high edge density. One can apply a stochastic blockmodel or modularity-based clustering to identify such clusters. For example, a simple model is: an edge $E_{ii'}\sim Bernoulli(p_{fraud})$ if both $i,i'$ are fraudulent, else $Bernoulli(p_{legit})$ for others. Using a latent-class graph model (degree-corrected SBM) one can estimate cluster membership. Implementing this in Python/Stan is involved: one could treat node memberships as latent discrete variables (like an LCA but on a graph) and use PyMC or an EM algorithm. However, if edges are rare outside fraud clusters, a spectral clustering (unsupervised) might suffice as a heuristic. Identifiability here relies on the assumption that fraudsters are much more interconnected than random; it can fail if ring members use unique proxies. No off-the-shelf Bayesian implementation exists for general graphs in Stan (some community models exist).  

5. **Conformal Prediction for Fraud Flags**. This is a recent idea: treat the problem as set prediction. Given a scoring function (e.g. from any classifier), use inductive conformal prediction to produce a set $S_\alpha$ of “flagged” responses with guaranteed error rate $\le\alpha$ under exchangeability assumptions. Concretely, split data into *calibration* and *test*. Fit any fraud-score model on calibration; then determine a threshold that yields at most $\alpha$ fraction of known valid cases flagged. Apply that threshold to flag new responses. The estimand is the conformal p-value or inclusion indicator. Implementation: one can use the `mapie` or `nonconformist` Python packages, or roll one’s own by sorting calibration scores. Identifiability is not an issue, but coverage guarantee holds only if calibration examples are exchangeable with test. Conformal sets are rigorous (unlike ad-hoc thresholds) and allow setting desired false-positive bounds, which is appealing when IRB mandates fairness.  

Each of these models could be coded in Python. For the Bayesian ones (LCM, mixture-IRT), frameworks like NumPyro allow vectorized HMC over latent classes. For the mixture models, one would write the log-likelihood integrating or sampling the latent class. For example, in NumPyro:

```python
# Pseudocode for latent-class model (2 tests example)
pi = numpyro.sample("pi", Beta(1,1))
p0 = numpyro.sample("p0", Beta(2,2), sample_shape=(2,))  # false-positive rates
p1 = numpyro.sample("p1", Beta(2,2), sample_shape=(2,))  # sensitivities
# z_i latent
with numpyro.plate("data", N):
    z = numpyro.sample("z", dist.Bernoulli(pi))
    for j in range(2):
        prob = numpyro.deterministic(f"prob_test{j}", jnp.where(z, p1[j], p0[j]))
        numpyro.sample(f"X{j}", dist.Bernoulli(prob), obs=X_obs[:,j])
```

This yields posterior draws of $\pi,p_{0,j},p_{1,j},z_i$. 

# Pilot Pipeline and Open Problems  

**Recommended pilot pipeline (~400 respondents):** 
(1) **Prevention:** Before fielding, employ reCAPTCHA or honeypot questions, and ensure the survey clearly states fraud-disclaimers (e.g. “no incentive if ineligible” as Teitcher *et al.* recommend). Use a screened consent to collect email/phone for follow-up. (2) **Recruitment controls:** Set Qualtrics to collect IP geolocation and enable its **Security Scan Monitor** and **Duplicate Detection** options. Disable multiple responses per device or mark duplicates with `Q_DuplicateRespondent`. (3) **Data collection:** Monitor paradata live (e.g. block IPs from abroad if target is US teens). After reaching ~400 completes, proceed to cleaning. (4) **Automated cleaning:** Apply multiple filters: e.g. remove anyone failing two or more of: rapid completion (<30% median), trivial trap questions (e.g. “select C”), repeated identical answers, and inconsistent screener/survey answers. (5) **Manual review:** For those with 1 flag, inspect open-ends: use SimHash or string-match to find duplicate or plagiarized text (Pinzón found exact repeats of survey language). (6) **Verification:** For borderline cases, send an email or SMS challenge asking them to recall a specific prior answer (Pinzón’s re-contact trick caught 99% of fraud but also showed fraudsters had retained their own entries). (7) **Model scoring:** Optionally, fit a simple Bayesian LCM or rule-based score as above to compute each respondent’s fraud probability. Choose a conservative threshold (e.g. auto-reject top 5% scores, manual review next 10%). (8) **Assessment:** Compute metrics: AUROC/PR (if any gold labels), but especially FPR by subgroup (ensure equal FPR for gender groups to avoid bias). Document thresholds used and number flagged at each stage. Use conformal methods to guarantee, say, ≤5% of true teens falsely excluded. 

**Open Problems:** The literature is thin on several fronts. Quantifying the real-world **prevalence** of fraud types for social-media-recruited minors remains unknown. Robust detection of **LLM-assisted human responses** is virtually unaddressed – current text detectors can be defeated by subtle prompt engineering (RAID findings). Effective **unsupervised fusion** (mixture models) needs more study: how to verify conditional independence of checks, and how many checks suffice? The impact of **privacy protections** (e.g. not storing device fingerprints or exact times for minors) on detectability is unclear. We lack **public benchmark datasets** of annotated survey fraud to rigorously evaluate methods. The interaction of fairness (e.g. ensuring that email/IP rules don’t exclude privacy-conscious or minority respondents) and fraud detection also needs theoretical work. Finally, adaptive adversaries (fraudsters who study and circumvent our rules) imply a cat-and-mouse game: methods for **continual learning and monitoring drift** are largely unexplored in this domain.

- Pinzón *et al.* (2024), *Front. Res. Metr. Anal.*: Survey of 31 fraud indicators in two incentive surveys; reports drastic fraud rates (usable dropped 75→10%), identifies top signals (email-score, MinFraud IP score, consecutive submissions, odd locations).  
- Comachio *et al.* (2025), *BMJ Evidence-Based Med.*: Scoping review of 23 studies; 78% encountered fraud. Fraudulent exclusions ranged 3–94%; 26% of studies used completion time (e.g. <5 min) to flag bots.  
- Panesar & Mayo (2023), *Can. Oncol. Nurs. J.*: “Taking out the trash” review of fraud in web-surveys; surveys should include honeypots, ID questions, paradata checks.  
- Pratt-Chapman *et al.* (2021), *JMIR Cancer*: Used ≥2-indicator rule to exclude fraud; removed 1,408 of 1,977 responses, altering sample demographics.  
- Pozzar *et al.* (2020), *JMIR*: Documented bot and farm threats via social-media ads; recommended time/IP checks (cited by others).  
- SoK by Ali *et al.* (SOUPS 2026): Systematizes survey-fraud threats; notes inconsistent definitions and that most defenses (attention/time checks) are applied *post hoc* and are often repurposed across threats.  
- Salinas (2023), *W. J. Nurs. Res.*: Noted pandemic-era fraud spikes; showed unrealistic completion times (Kramer 2014) catch many frauds.  
- Pinzón *et al.* (2024): Also manually used open-text analysis to verify fraud: early bots produced plagiarized, verbose answers; later fraud looked human-like but keying in duplications. Emailed respondents for re-check: 560 replies, 559 were fraudulent (they recalled some answers but revealed patterns).  
- Qualtrics Support (2024): Describes built-in Fraud Detection features: reCAPTCHA-based bot scoring and post-survey Duplicate Detection via device/IP. Notes that the older “RelevantID” tool was retired mid-2025.  
- CloudResearch Engage Newsletter (2025): Claims AI now evades standard checks (99.8% pass rate), advocates behavioral biometrics (mouse/typing) and reports their Sentry tool >99% detection vs AI (FPR <1%).  

(Italicized references are peer-reviewed unless noted as vendor material.)
